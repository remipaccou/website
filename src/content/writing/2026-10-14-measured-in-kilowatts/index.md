The fifth chapter of Norbert Wiener’s *Cybernetics*, published in 1948, sets computing machines beside the nervous system. Its last paragraph holds a sentence that has had a long life of its own: “Information is information, not matter or energy.” The sentence is quoted in histories of computing, in essays on the digital economy and in introductions to the philosophy of information, nearly always on its own. The paragraph around it is about heat.

> As a final remark, let me point out that a large computing machine, whether in the form of mechanical or electric apparatus or in the form of the brain itself, uses up a considerable amount of power, all of which is wasted and dissipated in heat. The blood leaving the brain is a fraction of a degree warmer than that entering it. No other computing machine approaches the economy of energy of the brain. In a large apparatus like the Eniac or Edvac, the filaments of the tubes consume a quantity of energy which may well be measured in kilowatts, and unless adequate ventilating and cooling apparatus is provided, the system will suffer from what is the mechanical equivalent of pyrexia, until the constants of the machine are radically changed by the heat, and its performance breaks down. Nevertheless, the energy spent per individual operation is almost vanishingly small, and does not even begin to form an adequate measure of the performance of the apparatus. The mechanical brain does not secrete thought “as the liver does bile,” as the earlier materialists claimed, nor does it put it out in the form of energy, as the muscle puts out its activity. Information is information, not matter or energy. No materialism which does not admit this can survive at the present day.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup>

Wiener knew the kilowatts were there. He wrote of ventilation and cooling, and he gave the overheated machine a physician’s word, pyrexia, a fever. What he refused was something else: that the energy a machine consumes should be taken as the measure of what it does. The power drawn by the filaments says nothing of the problem being solved, and the same kilowatts keep the tubes warm whether the machine is computing a firing table or waiting for the next one.

The last line of the paragraph is addressed to materialism, and it is not a farewell to it. The “earlier materialists” are those of the nineteenth century: the comparison Wiener quotes goes back to Carl Vogt, for whom, in the 1840s, thought stood to the brain as bile stands to the liver. Wiener sets aside that picture, and the one in which thought would be an output of energy, as work is the output of a muscle. He does not set aside materialism. He asks it to admit a third term beside matter and energy, one that neither of them measures. Read with its paragraph, the sentence does not say that information has left the physical world. It says that within that world there are two quantities, and that the size of the one does not give the size of the other.

## A machine that had to be cooled

The ENIAC had been ordered in 1943 by the United States Army’s Ordnance Department from the Moore School of the University of Pennsylvania, to compute the firing tables the artillery was waiting for. It was presented in February 1946, too late for the war that had paid for it, and ran at Aberdeen until October 1955. Its eighteen thousand vacuum tubes drew well over a hundred kilowatts, 130 according to the Army’s own history of the machine, and forced cooling was needed to keep about five thousand additions per second going.<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> Electricity was no longer an auxiliary of the office, as it had been for the punched-card machines of the interwar years. Its stability decided whether a calculation came out whole, and the heat it left behind decided whether the machine could keep running.

Those who built such machines knew it, and designed around it. At Bell Laboratories, the solid-state physics group formed in 1945, from which the transistor would come two years later, was set up in part to have done with the vacuum tube: its dissipation, its heating, its fragility.<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> In the SAGE air-defence system of the following decade, the computer, its cooling and its power supply were conceived together. The Air Force had the buildings built to IBM’s specifications, with a power supply of their own, and the documented load ranges from 0.75 megawatt, IBM’s figure for the duplex computer, to about 1.8 megawatts for an installation whose computer and air conditioning were measured together.<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>

The power is there, in the specifications, from the first machine onwards. It is absent from the documents through which these machines entered economic life: the contract and its price, the hours of computation, the performance in operations per second. Energy became visible to the engineer long before it became visible to the economist.

## Two quantities and no rate of exchange

Wiener’s distinction was sound, and it has held. In the same year, Claude Shannon defined information without reference to its meaning and gave it a unit, the bit, which measures a message whatever carries it.<sup><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup> In 1961 Rolf Landauer showed that the logically irreversible erasure of a bit dissipates at least $kT\ln 2$ of energy.<sup><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup> Information always has a physical realisation, then, and that realisation has a floor. But the floor lies many orders of magnitude below what any real machine consumes, as [an earlier essay on this site](../../../../2025/12/12/the-thermodynamic-limits-of-terrestrial-computational-intelligence/) discusses at length; it explains neither the kilowatts of the ENIAC nor the industry that followed. Between the bound and the machine stand the components, the architectures and the uses. There is no rate at which kilowatt-hours convert into information, and Wiener was right to keep the two apart.

A category does not only describe, however. It also sorts what will be measured, and by whom. Once information is defined as what energy does not measure, the energy of information can be left to others: to the engineer, the building manager, the electricity supplier. Katherine Hayles has traced how, in the decades that followed cybernetics, information came to be thought of as separable from the material forms that carry it.<sup><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> Nathan Ensmenger has shown how the vocabulary of immateriality helped keep the buildings, the cooling, the electricity and the waste of computing out of measurement.<sup><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup> Wiener’s sentence lent itself to that sorting; the paragraph around it does not.

The figure follows the energy of computing in two places from 1946 onwards. In the specifications it is present from the start, and what the electricity does changes over time. In the ENIAC it executes the operation. With semiconductor memory, from 1970, it also holds a state that vanishes when the current stops: such memory has to be refreshed many times a second, whether or not anyone is computing. With the Web, from the early 1990s, it holds availability: what is sold is a server that answers at any hour, and machines are kept running for requests that may or may not come.

In the accounts the path is slower. Over the same decades the economy learned to count computing as capital. The System of National Accounts of 1993 recorded software as a fixed asset when it is used in production for more than a year;<sup><a href="#ref-9">9</a></sup> nothing in that decision attached an energy to the asset. From 1992 the Energy Star label, which the Environmental Protection Agency launched with computers, monitors and printers, set out to bring the power-saving features of laptops to the machines on the desk.<sup><a href="#ref-10">10</a></sup> It measured the machine on the desk, at the very time computing was moving behind the network.

The first official count of computing as a service came in August 2007. At the request of Congress, the same agency estimated that American data centres had consumed 61 terawatt-hours in 2006, 1.5 per cent of the country’s electricity, twice as much as in 2000, and that the figure could nearly double again by 2011.<sup><a href="#ref-11">11</a></sup> Estimates had circulated before, some of them far off the mark, and one, funded by a chip maker, a few months earlier; this one was required by law and published by a federal agency. In the same year an industry consortium proposed a metric, the ratio between the total energy of a data centre and that of its computing equipment alone, which made one site comparable with another.<sup><a href="#ref-12">12</a></sup> The projection was later revised downwards: growth between 2005 and 2010 turned out to be about half of what had been expected.<sup><a href="#ref-13">13</a></sup> What matters here is less the precision of these numbers than the fact that they exist. The energy of information now had an official measure, with a unit, a perimeter and a purpose, instead of being reconstructed after the event.

Sixty-one years separate the kilowatts of the ENIAC from that report. The interval is not the time it took to discover a hidden fact. The fact was printed in the specifications, and Wiener wrote it down in 1948. It is the time it took for a quantity that engineers handled every day to become a quantity that an economy keeps track of.

## The narrative and the axis

Near the end of *The Question Concerning Technology in China*, Yuk Hui distinguishes two things that are easily taken for one another: the history that a civilisation tells as a narrative, and the way that history works materially, through an axis of time that synchronises activity across the globe and grows more powerful and more homogeneous as it does. Europe may declare the end of modernity; that does not bring the axis to a stop.<sup><a href="#ref-14">14</a></sup> The rich economies have announced the end of their industrial age several times, under the names of post-industrial society, information society, dematerialisation. Meanwhile the foundries, the assembly lines, the cables and the data centres went on, often elsewhere, and their consumption grew.

Wiener’s sentence has often been lent to these narratives of ending, as though it said that information had left matter behind. His paragraph says something more exact. The machine heats, it has to be cooled, its consumption is large, and that consumption is not the measure of what it does. Both halves hold. For six decades the first remained with the engineers while the economy took up the second, and the two were seldom read together.

The energy of computing is now counted, region by region, in the reports of the International Energy Agency and in the plans of electricity system operators.<sup><a href="#ref-15">15</a></sup> What remains open is whether the cost of that energy reaches those who decide how much computing will be built, and where.
